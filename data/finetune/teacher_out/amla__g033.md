## 2. Product, Market & Value Chain

### Introduction
Guava juice is a nutritious, gently processed fruit beverage made from ripe guavas, prized for their fragrant aroma, distinctive flavor, and naturally high vitamin and antioxidant content. The proposed unit by Chamorshi Guava Farmers Producer Company Ltd in Gadchiroli, Maharashtra will source fresh guavas from member orchards, convert them into a stable, safe juice through hygienic washing, seed removal and pulping, clarification or fine filtration as required, pasteurization, and packaging. Permitted preservatives such as potassium metabisulphite may be used within regulatory limits to enhance shelf stability, while maintaining a fruit-forward profile. The product can be offered as ready-to-drink juice, nectar-style beverage, or as a base for blended fruit beverages, depending on market preferences and regulatory definitions.

Raw materials include fully mature guava fruit of suitable cultivars, potable water for dilution and process needs, food-grade additives when necessary for flavor balance and preservation, and primary and secondary packaging materials. The line configuration, quality control protocols, and sanitation procedures are designed to protect natural sensory attributes while ensuring compliance with food-safety standards.

### Uses & Benefits
- A refreshing beverage for daily consumption, breakfast accompaniment, and on-the-go hydration.
- Naturally rich in vitamin C and dietary fiber, supporting immunity and digestive wellness.
- Suitable as a base for smoothies, mocktails, and culinary sauces, expanding foodservice applications.
- Creates a reliable outlet for seasonal gluts of fruit, helping stabilize farm incomes and reduce post-harvest losses.
- Local, traceable sourcing reassures consumers seeking origin-assured, minimally processed fruit beverages.

### Market Potential
Demand for fruit-based beverages continues to expand across urban and semi-urban India, driven by interest in natural, nutritious drinks and a shift from synthetic soft drinks toward fruit-forward options. Guava has strong consumer familiarity and regional acceptance, and blends well with other fruits, enabling multiple stock-keeping units aligned to varying sweetness and pulp preferences. Institutional and foodservice segments appreciate guava’s robust flavor and stability in chillers and dispensers. In the Vidarbha belt and neighboring regions, local-brand loyalty and farm-to-bottle narratives offer a credible edge, while e-commerce and quick-commerce channels support year-round access and discovery.

### Value Chain
- Farm-level production: Member farmers adopt good orchard practices, harvest at physiological maturity, and deliver fruit to designated collection points.
- Primary handling: At the FPO facility, fruit is weighed, sorted, and washed to remove field dirt and surface microbial load.
- Seed removal and pulping: Guavas are cut or segmented, seeds are separated, and the pulp is prepared using a pulper/finisher to achieve target texture.
- Blending and standardization: Potable water is added for the intended juice style; additives such as acidity regulators, sugar, and KMS may be dosed as per standards and product design.
- Filtration and pasteurization: The beverage is clarified or fine-filtered as per the product brief, pasteurized to ensure safety and stability, then cooled.
- Filling and packaging: Hygienic filling into bottles or other approved containers is followed by capping, coding, labeling, and secondary packing.
- Storage and distribution: Finished goods are stored under recommended conditions and dispatched via distributors, modern trade, HoReCa, institutions, and digital channels.
- By-products and waste: Seeds and peels can be valorized for feed, pectin-rich applications, or bio-enzymes; process water can be treated and reused where permissible.

### Competition & Marketing
The category features national beverage brands, regional fruit processors, and fresh-pressed juice sellers. The FPO can differentiate through farm-traceability, minimal ingredient lists, authentic guava taste, and community impact. Marketing levers include:
- Local-origin storytelling and seasonal limited editions tied to member orchards.
- Assured quality through transparent processing content and clean labeling.
- Multi-channel placement: kirana stores, modern retail, foodservice, institutional buyers, and online partners.
- Sampling-led activations at community events, health fairs, and tourism nodes.
- Co-branded packs with allied local produce to build a regional identity.

### Risk & Mitigation
- Raw material seasonality and quality variability: Buffer procurement plans, staggered harvests, pre-graded lots, and cold staging where feasible.
- Price volatility: Forward procurement agreements and diversified sourcing across member clusters.
- Food safety and compliance: Robust SOPs, prerequisite programs, HACCP-based controls, and in-house QA testing.
- Utility disruptions: Backup power, preventive maintenance, and calibrated heat-treatment controls.
- Packaging and logistics: Approved suppliers, pre-dispatch inspections, and route optimization for monsoon and rural road conditions.
- Market acceptance: Pilot batches, iterative sensory panels, and nimble SKU strategy to align pulpiness and sweetness preferences.

### Employment & Socio-Economic Impact
The unit creates steady employment for local youth and women across sorting, processing, quality assurance, packing, warehousing, and last-mile logistics. Stable offtake of guavas improves farmgate realization, reduces distress sales, and builds resilience against crop losses. Skill development in hygiene, equipment operation, and basic lab practices enhances employability. Community nutrition awareness can be woven into brand initiatives, strengthening local goodwill.

### Sustainability
Sustainability measures include efficient water use and recovery, closed-loop CIP, and responsible effluent treatment. Renewable or cleaner energy options can reduce the plant’s footprint. By-product utilization minimizes organic waste. Recyclable or reusable packaging and responsible sourcing policies support circularity. On-farm practices such as mulching, integrated pest management, and soil-health stewardship further improve the value chain’s environmental performance.
