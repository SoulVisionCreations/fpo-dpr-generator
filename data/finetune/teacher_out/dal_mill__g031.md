## 2. Product, Market & Value Chain

### Introduction
Dal is a staple food product made by milling pulses, especially chickpea, to remove the husk and split the cotyledon into clean, uniform kernels suitable for cooking. As the principal plant-based protein in Indian diets, dal also contributes valuable dietary fibre, iron and B‑vitamins. This DPR proposes a gram-based dal mill for Ranchi Urad Dal Producer Company Ltd, located at Sikidiri, Ormanjhi, Ranchi, Jharkhand, leveraging proximity to pulse-growing belts and farmer members for assured, quality raw material and improved producer realisations.

### Uses & Benefits
Dal is consumed daily in households across regions and cuisines, and is also a core ingredient for ready-to-cook mixes, snacks, namkeen, and institutional meals. Benefits extend beyond nutrition to culinary versatility, long shelf-life when properly milled and packed, and quick cooking when the split is uniform. For the FPO, dal milling enables value addition at the source, reduction of post-harvest losses, better price discovery for farmers, and creation of a consistent, branded product for local and regional markets.

### Market Potential
The demand for pulses and packaged dal continues to rise with growing health awareness, urbanisation, preference for clean-label staples, and expansion of modern retail and e-commerce. Government nutrition and safety net programmes, the HORECA channel, and rising demand for convenient yet traditional foods all support steady offtake. Jharkhand and adjoining states offer a natural home market with scope for premiumisation through certified quality, hygienic processing, and traceability from farmer groups to finished packs.

### Value Chain
The dal value chain begins with aggregation of chickpea from farmer members and local mandi linkages. After scientific storage, raw pulses undergo soaking, gentle drying, pre-cleaning to remove impurities, de-husking using emery systems, separation of husk, splitting, polishing to the targeted finish, grading for uniformity, and packing for dispatch. Quality control is embedded at every stage, ensuring moisture and defect management, food-safety compliance, and batch traceability. By-products such as husk and brokens are monetised for feed or other uses, enhancing overall recovery and value realisation. The chain culminates in wholesale, retail and institutional distribution, supported by brand building and market development by the FPO.

### Competition & Marketing
Competition spans local unbranded mills and large branded players with wide retail penetration. The FPO will differentiate through farmer-sourced origin, freshness from short supply chains, rigorous cleaning and grading, food-safety aligned processes, and honest packaging declarations. Marketing will focus on traditional retail in and around Ranchi, partnerships with kirana networks, institutional buyers, and online marketplaces. Consumer education around nutrition, regional taste profiles, and cooking performance will reinforce brand trust. Participation in government procurement, where eligible, can supplement market channels and stabilise throughput.

### Risk & Mitigation
Key risks include raw material price volatility and quality variation, storage losses due to pests or moisture, equipment downtime, power interruptions, and market price pressures. Mitigation measures encompass planned procurement aligned to harvest windows, supplier tie-ups with clear quality norms, scientific storage and fumigation practices, preventive maintenance, dust and waste control systems, energy back-up and efficiency initiatives, diversified customer base, prudent working-capital planning, and strict compliance with food safety standards and labeling regulations.

### Employment & Socio-Economic Impact
The mill generates local employment in operations, logistics, quality control, administration and sales, with strong potential for women’s participation in grading, packing and documentation. Farmer members benefit from assured and transparent procurement, better returns linked to quality, and capacity building in post-harvest handling. Local services such as transport, packaging, and maintenance gain steady business, creating a broader rural economic multiplier. Reliable access to hygienically processed dal also contributes to household nutrition security in the region.

### Sustainability
Sustainability priorities include efficient energy use, adoption of low-dust handling and baghouse filtration, responsible water management for soaking and cleaning with reuse where feasible, and eco-conscious packaging choices. Husk and other by-products can be valorised for feed or thermal applications, reducing waste. Rainwater harvesting, rooftop solar readiness, and good manufacturing practices lower the environmental footprint. Continuous training and monitoring ensure occupational health, safety and community well-being.
