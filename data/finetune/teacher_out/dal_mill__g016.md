## 2. Product, Market & Value Chain

### Introduction
Dal is the split and dehusked form of pulses such as chickpea, an essential protein source in Indian diets and integral to daily meals across regions. The proposed unit of Mangkolemba Agro Producer Company Ltd in Mangkolemba, Mokokchung, Nagaland, will mill gram into high-quality chana dal through a standardized sequence of soaking, drying, cleaning, de-husking, splitting, polishing, and packing. Locating the mill within a pulse-growing belt enables direct farm-gate procurement, lower logistics costs, fresher raw material, and transparent price discovery for member farmers. The enterprise aligns with value addition goals under cluster-based food processing initiatives and strengthens local agrarian livelihoods.

### Uses & Benefits
Chana dal is widely used in home cooking, institutional kitchens, restaurants, and food processing applications, prized for its taste, nutrition, and versatility. It is rich in protein, dietary fibre, and key micronutrients, supporting balanced diets. For farmers, community-level milling adds value, reduces post-harvest losses, and diversifies market options. For consumers, standardized cleaning and grading deliver consistent cooking quality and improved food safety. By-products such as husk and brokens can be gainfully utilized in feed or allied sectors, creating additional revenue streams and waste minimization. The model promotes inclusive growth by linking primary producers to end markets under a farmer-owned brand.

### Market Potential
Pulses remain a staple in Indian cuisine, with stable demand across income segments and steady penetration of branded and packaged formats through retail chains and e-commerce. Chana dal commands year-round consumption in households and sees steady draw from hostels, hospitals, community kitchens, defence messes, and quick-service outlets. In the Northeast, the combination of local production and inter-state trade creates opportunities for regionally branded dal that emphasizes freshness, origin, and traceability. Demand is also supported by wellness trends that favour plant proteins, clean labels, and minimally processed foods. The business can diversify over time into unpolished variants and consumer-ready packs to capture value in both wholesale and retail channels.

### Value Chain
The value chain spans aggregation of whole gram from member farmers, quality assessment and cleaning, moisture conditioning through soaking and drying, dehusking in emery roll systems, precision splitting, polishing as per market preference, grading, and final packing for dispatch. Close coordination with growers ensures varietal purity and pre-harvest advisory, while proper storage maintains grain integrity. Standard operating procedures and batch-wise testing help manage moisture, foreign matter, and appearance benchmarks. Husk and screenings are segregated for sale to feed users or for energy substitution in thermal applications, enhancing overall resource efficiency. Finished goods move through wholesalers, institutional buyers, and retail networks, with the FPO brand emphasising farmer ownership and local provenance.

### Competition & Marketing
The competitive set includes established dal mills in central and western zones and regional players supplying the Northeast. Differentiation for the FPO rests on traceability to Nagaland producers, transparent quality standards, and reliable service to trade partners. The marketing mix will combine bulk sales to wholesalers and institutions with consumer packs supported by simple, honest branding that foregrounds nutrition, origin, and farmer benefit. Participation in government procurement programmes, tie-ups with cooperatives and self-help group distribution, and digital commerce can broaden reach. Trade relationships will be reinforced through consistent quality, timely supply, fair credit practices, and after-sales feedback loops.

### Risk & Mitigation
- Raw material volatility: Addressed through staggered procurement, storage planning, supplier diversification, and value addition to by-products.
- Quality variability: Managed by pre-season agronomy support, intake testing, calibrated soaking and drying, and robust in-process controls.
- Operational disruptions: Mitigated with preventive maintenance, spare-parts planning, trained operators, and backup power arrangements.
- Market and price risk: Reduced via balanced customer mix, forward bookings with institutions, and focus on differentiated, traceable branding.
- Compliance and food safety: Ensured by systematic hygiene protocols, staff training, and adherence to applicable food standards and labelling norms.

### Employment & Socio-Economic Impact
The project generates local jobs in operations, maintenance, quality control, logistics, and sales, with opportunities for women and youth in sorting, packaging, and administration. It promotes stable incomes for member farmers through assured market access and shared value, alongside ancillary livelihoods in transport and services. Skills in grain handling, milling technology, and small enterprise management are strengthened through hands-on training and mentoring. Community visibility of a farmer-owned brand builds pride and encourages wider participation in organized value chains.

### Sustainability
Resource-efficient practices such as controlled water use in soaking, heat recovery and efficient drying, and dust suppression contribute to a cleaner operation. Husk and biomass residues can be channelled to thermal applications or sold to feed users, reducing waste. The unit can progressively adopt renewable energy solutions and eco-friendly packaging options while promoting responsible procurement and storage that limit losses. Transparent traceability and fair payments embed social sustainability, while climate-smart agronomy advisories support resilient pulse production in the region.
